# Signal-level quality control and mastering for TTS takes (state as of November 2026)

Scope: per-take signal checks (quality predictors, artifacts, speaker consistency), restoration, loudness and leveling, and the join, for Breeze TTS 2 takes heard in a car and at low volume in bed. Dropped or repeated words, pronunciation and prosody are out of scope and are not covered here.

Context fact used throughout: Breeze TTS 2 outputs 24 kHz mono signed 16-bit PCM. Its audio tokenizer is based on the Qwen3-TTS tokenizer, which runs at 12.5 frames per second (80 ms per frame) with 16 residual codebooks. Details and sources are under question 6.

## 1. Which non-intrusive quality predictors exist, how well do they catch a glitch in one utterance, and what are their sizes, CPU speed, ONNX availability and licences?

### Takeaway
Every MOS-style predictor ranks systems well but individual utterances poorly (utterance-level SRCC about 0.4 to 0.6 even for the best 2024 system), so none of them reliably flags a single glitched take on its own. They are useful as relative scores within one voice (best-of-N picking, outlier flags against the voice's own median), and the only tool built to localize a bad segment inside one TTS utterance is the frame-level SQA model from Paderborn (fgnt/local_sqa, ICASSP 2026), which still has recall around 0.4.

### Cited Findings

Utterance-level vs system-level accuracy
- UTMOSv2 (team T05, VoiceMOS Challenge 2024 Track 1) on the 25% zoom-in evaluation set scored utterance-level SRCC 0.613 and LCC 0.618 but system-level SRCC 0.919. On the harder 12% set it scored utterance SRCC 0.579 vs system SRCC 0.854. The original UTMOS scored utterance SRCC 0.411 and 0.300 on the same sets - [UTMOSv2 paper, arXiv 2409.09305](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09305v1)
- The SOMOS dataset paper found system-level correlations high while utterance-level correlations stayed consistently low, even for systems seen in training - [SOMOS, arXiv 2204.03040](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03040)
- Kuhlmann et al. (Interspeech 2025) found that some predictors (SHEET SSL-MOS, similar to UTMOS) give nearly constant frame-level scores, which harms localized-distortion detection, and they proposed chunk-based processing so a local distortion does not spill into neighbouring frames. Their out-of-domain utterance-level SRCC for SSL-MOS was about 0.49 in their distortion setup - [Towards Frame-level Quality Predictions of Synthetic Speech, arXiv 2508.10374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10374), [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.10374)
- TTSDS2 (Minixhofer, Klejch, Bell, arXiv June 2025, v2 March 2026) is the only one of 16 compared metrics with Spearman above 0.50 across all domains, averaging about 0.67 and never exceeding 0.8. It is a distribution-level benchmark score - [TTSDS2, arXiv 2506.19441](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.19441v2)
- Generative speech enhancement can hallucinate wrong content or drift speaker identity, and summaries of that literature say these errors are not reliably caught by DNSMOS, UTMOS, ASR confidence or CTC score - [PASE, AAAI 2026](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/40562/44523), [arXiv 2511.13300 as summarized in search, full text not read](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.13300v1)

Frame-level localization inside one TTS utterance
- Kuhlmann, Werning, von Neumann, Haeb-Umbach (Paderborn, arXiv 29 January 2026, ICASSP 2026): a WavLM Large encoder plus a 1-layer BLSTM decoder trained only on utterance MOS labels with two consistency losses gives one score per ~20 ms frame. Frames below a threshold set so only 1% of human test-clean frames fall under it are flagged, then smoothed with a 200 ms window and detections under 100 ms dropped - [arXiv 2601.21886](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21886v1)
- On PartialSpoof the same model reached precision 0.628, recall 0.404, F1 0.492 (strict criterion), against F1 0.725 for the supervised BAM baseline. On real voice clones (about 10,000 utterances each from StyleTTS2 and F5-TTS) the flagged StyleTTS2 segments were mostly failed synthesis (non-speech) and low audio quality such as low SNR, clipping or buzzing. Listeners judged flagged segments human-like roughly 28 to 42% of the time vs about 65 to 80% for random control segments. It also flags laughter and other non-verbal sounds. Code: github.com/fgnt/local_sqa - [arXiv 2601.21886](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21886v1)

Tool facts
- UTMOSv2: predicts naturalness MOS of high-quality synthetic speech. Architecture is EfficientNetV2 CNNs on mel spectrograms at several window sizes fused with wav2vec 2.0 base SSL features, a 5-fold ensemble with 5 test-time passes of random frames each. Licence MIT, input sr defaults to 16 kHz, accepted at IEEE SLT 2024, first in 7 of 16 VMC 2024 Track 1 metrics and second in the other 9. No ONNX export or CPU speed is documented - [GitHub sarulab-speech/UTMOSv2](https://github.com/sarulab-speech/UTMOSv2), [arXiv 2409.09305](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09305v1)
- The UTMOSv2 Hugging Face Space stores 4.09 GB in its models folder (Space storage, not one checkpoint) - [HF Space tree](https://huggingface.co/spaces/sarulab-speech/UTMOSv2/tree/main/models)
- Distill-MOS (Microsoft, Stahl and Gamper, ICASSP 2025, released October 2024): a convolutional transformer distilled from an XLS-R wav2vec 2.0 teacher (XLS-R-SQA) retrained on over 100,000 labelled clips, about two orders of magnitude smaller than the teacher, halving the correlation gap between baseline and teacher. Input mono 16 kHz in 7.68 s segments, output MOS 1 to 5, MIT licence, pip install distillmos. Training data includes Coqui TTS outputs, VoiceMOS 2022 and NISQA, and the card says it is aimed mainly at enhancement and degradation scenarios in English - [GitHub microsoft/Distill-MOS](https://github.com/microsoft/Distill-MOS), [arXiv 2502.05356](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2502.05356), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/distillmos/)
- SCOREQ (Ragano, Skoglund, Hines, NeurIPS 2024): a triplet-loss contrastive regression that replaces L2 loss to improve generalisation, with consistent gains across 11 datasets. Four modes: natural or synthetic speech, each no-reference or non-matching-reference. MIT licence, pip install scoreq, weights on Zenodo (v2 published 25 June 2025), and the repo recommends trimming input to 10 to 15 s to avoid memory problems - [arXiv 2410.06675](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06675), [PyPI scoreq](https://pypi.org/project/scoreq/), [GitHub alessandroragano/scoreq](https://github.com/alessandroragano/scoreq), [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/15739280)
- Meta Audiobox Aesthetics (paper 7 February 2025): predicts four axes, Production Quality (PQ), Production Complexity (PC), Content Enjoyment (CE), Content Usefulness (CU). About 0.1B parameters, F32 safetensors, WavLM-derived code, licence CC-BY 4.0 with MIT parts, pip install audiobox_aesthetics with a batch CLI (audio-aes) that writes one JSON line per file and accepts start and end times - [HF facebook/audiobox-aesthetics](https://huggingface.co/facebook/audiobox-aesthetics), [arXiv 2502.05139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05139), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/audiobox-aesthetics/)
- TorchAudio SQUIM: SquimObjective estimates STOI, PESQ and SI-SDR with no reference (encoder, DPRNN, one branch per metric). The tutorial shows estimates close to true values, for example at 20 dB SNR STOI 0.961 vs 0.967, PESQ 2.78 vs 2.80, SI-SDR 20.7 vs 20.0 dB. SquimSubjective predicts MOS - [SQUIM tutorial](https://pytorch.org/audio/2.1/tutorials/squim_tutorial.html), [SquimObjective docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/audio/stable/generated/torchaudio.models.SquimObjective.html)
- DNSMOS P.835: predicts SIG, BAK and OVRL, distributed as ONNX (sig_bak_ovr.onnx with model_v8.onnx for P.808). The paper reports PCC 0.94 for SIG and 0.98 for BAK and OVRL against human ratings - [DNSMOS P.835, arXiv 2110.01763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01763)
- TorchMetrics wraps DNSMOS through onnxruntime with a num_threads option for CPU, output order [p808_mos, mos_sig, mos_bak, mos_ovr] - [TorchMetrics DNSMOS](https://lightning.ai/docs/torchmetrics/stable/audio/deep_noise_suppression_mean_opinion_score.html)
- A torch-native DNSMOS port has MIT code and CC BY 4.0 weights and warns that non-16 kHz input is resampled differently from the reference - [HF prj-beatrice/dnsmos-torch-native](https://huggingface.co/prj-beatrice/dnsmos-torch-native)
- The LocalVQE team reported DNSMOS scores contradicting listening tests on near-silent output - [LocalVQE model card commit](https://huggingface.co/LocalAI-io/LocalVQE/commit/29a5a0d5c0d5c77e1a21df844ee574eff3a3f942)
- The Emilia TTS-data pipeline keeps only segments with DNSMOS P.835 OVRL above 3.0 (with IQR outlier removal on character duration), raising mean DNSMOS to 3.26 - [Emilia, arXiv 2407.05361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05361), [Emilia extended, arXiv 2501.15907](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15907)
- NISQA v2.0 (Interspeech 2021) predicts overall quality plus noisiness, coloration, discontinuity and loudness dimensions, and a separate NISQA-TTS model predicts naturalness. A third-party ONNX export reports MIT code and CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 weights - [arXiv 2104.09494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09494), [HF TigreGotico/nisqa-onnx](https://huggingface.co/TigreGotico/nisqa-onnx), [GitHub gabrielmittag/NISQA](https://github.com/gabrielmittag/NISQA)

### Inferences
- For audiogen the realistic use of MOS predictors is relative: score every take of a voice, and act on takes far below that voice's own median (for example more than 2 to 3 MAD below), or render N seeds and keep the best. An absolute threshold such as Emilia's DNSMOS OVRL 3.0 is a data-cleaning heuristic for real recordings and has not been validated on clean zero-shot TTS.
- Cheapest CPU options: DNSMOS (ONNX, tiny, already runnable with sherpa-onnx's onnxruntime dependency or plain onnxruntime) and Distill-MOS (small, 16 kHz). UTMOSv2 is the most accurate on synthetic speech but is a 5-fold by 5-pass ensemble with an EfficientNet plus wav2vec 2.0 front end, so it is the slowest on CPU. SCOREQ's synthetic no-reference mode is the only one whose training targets synthetic speech explicitly besides UTMOSv2 and NISQA-TTS.
- The fgnt/local_sqa frame scores map directly to an automatic fix: a flagged region inside a take means take the line again with a new seed. Because it uses WavLM Large (about 300M parameters, my estimate from the WavLM paper family, not stated in the source) it is a GPU or slow-CPU tool, best run on the Mac or on the laptop between renders.
- Audiobox Aesthetics PQ could rank takes of one line, but nothing published shows it catches short glitches.

### Gaps
- No published CPU latency for UTMOSv2, Distill-MOS, SCOREQ or DNSMOS sig_bak_ovr.onnx was found. These need timing on the M1 and the laptop CPU.
- WVMOS and the SHEET SSL-MOS variants were not researched in detail beyond the mention in arXiv 2508.10374.
- Exact parameter count of Distill-MOS students and of the local_sqa model were not stated on the pages read.
- I found no study that measures glitch detection rate of these predictors on codec-language-model TTS like Breeze.
- Whether SquimSubjective requires a non-matching clean reference was not confirmed on the pages read.

## 2. How are specific artifacts detected (vocoder and codec buzz, noise bursts, clipping, hum, breaths, cut-off final phoneme, changes of reverberation or bandwidth), and how is per-voice outlier detection done?

### Takeaway
There is no mature open detector for neural-codec "metallic" artifacts in TTS. The practical kit is a mix of DSP checks (clipping, DC, hum, bandwidth, edge energy), per-frame estimators of SNR and reverberation (Brouhaha), small CNN artifact classifiers (LAION, trained on synthetic corruptions only) and the frame-level SQA above, all compared against the same voice's other takes rather than absolute thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- LAION speech-artifact-detectors (2025, Apache 2.0, PyTorch .pt only, 16 kHz mono, up to 10 s): 10 binary CNNs of about 1.0 to 1.9M parameters each, for vocoder artifacts, spectral-gating "metallic or underwater" sound, pitch correction, MP3 codec warbling, comb filtering, phase-vocoder smearing, bandwidth limitation (lowpass 2 to 6 kHz) and clipping. Validation accuracy 97.4 to 100% on the authors' own synthetic corruptions, no cross-dataset testing. The stft_classifier scores about 1.0 for all vocoder-synthesized audio, so it cannot separate good from bad TTS. Clipping was the hardest task (98.33%) because soft clipping overlaps natural harmonics. The codec detector was trained on MP3 round-trips, not neural codecs. The card says inference is fast enough for real time on CPU but gives no benchmark - [HF laion/speech-artifact-detectors](https://huggingface.co/laion/speech-artifact-detectors)
- The frame-level SQA model flagged StyleTTS2 segments that were non-speech, low SNR, clipped or buzzing - [arXiv 2601.21886](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21886v1)
- TTS-PRISM, a Mandarin diagnostic framework, defines its worst artifact level as frequent popping and metallic artifacts that hinder intelligibility - [TTS-PRISM, arXiv 2604.22225](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.22225)
- A January 2026 study of audio deepfake detection found hierarchical fusion models capture autoregressive TTS artifacts but falter on flow-matching systems, showing artifact signatures differ by TTS architecture - [arXiv 2601.20510](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20510)
- Brouhaha (pyannote, ASRU 2023): one model gives per-frame (16 ms) speech probability, SNR and C50 (clarity, a reverberation measure) from single-channel audio, trained on synthetic noise and reverberation. A secondary summary reports SNR mean absolute error of 2.3 dB on unseen synthetic audio. The HF model is gated and needs pyannote.audio plus brouhaha-vad - [HF pyannote/brouhaha](https://huggingface.co/pyannote/brouhaha), [arXiv 2210.13248](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13248)
- ffmpeg adeclip replaces clipped samples by autoregressive interpolation, detecting clips with a histogram (defaults window 55 ms, overlap 75%, arorder 8%, threshold 10, hsize 1000). adeclick does the same for impulsive noise (defaults window 55 ms, arorder 2%, threshold 2, burst 2%) - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- ffmpeg silencedetect logs silence at or below a noise tolerance (default -60 dB) for at least a minimum duration (default 2 s), with lavfi.silence_start, silence_end and silence_duration metadata - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- Breath removal: an attention U-Net of 1.9M parameters detects and removes breath sounds, trained on DAPS (arXiv September 2024). No released code was confirmed - [arXiv 2409.04949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04949)
- iZotope RX Breath Control distinguishes breaths by their harmonic structure and can reduce them by a fixed gain or to a target level. A third-party article says the RX 12 version is machine-learning based with a target such as -55 LUFS - [iZotope RX 8 Leveler help](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx8/en/leveler/index.html), [RX 12 article](https://www.podcastvideos.com/izotope-rx-12-breath-control-module-update/)
- Breeze TTS 2's card says reference audio should contain clean speech with minimal background noise - [HF BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2](https://huggingface.co/BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2)
- Breeze-TTS-2.cpp's depth decoder runs 15 sequential steps for every audio frame, and the vocoder lives in the audio tokenizer - [HF HoppouAI/Breeze-TTS-2.cpp](https://huggingface.co/HoppouAI/Breeze-TTS-2.cpp)
- The Qwen3-TTS tokenizer runs at 12.5 Hz (80 ms frames), 24 kHz in and out, with 16 residual VQ codebooks (first semantic, 15 residual), about 2.2 kbps (secondary sources and a config file) - [tokenizer config.json](https://huggingface.co/simone00/italian_multi/blob/main/speech_tokenizer/config.json), [Emergent Mind summary](https://api.emergentmind.com/topics/qwen3-tts-series), [Qwen3-TTS report, arXiv 2601.15621](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15621)
- Emilia's pipeline removes outliers with an IQR rule on average character duration, an example of robust per-population outlier rejection - [Emilia extended, arXiv 2501.15907](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15907)

### Inferences
- The 15-step depth decoder and 16 codebooks imply that one bad token frame corrupts about 80 ms of audio. A buzzy or metallic stretch should therefore show up as a run of 80 ms-aligned frames with abnormal spectral flatness or harmonic-to-noise ratio, distinct from the sub-1 ms bursts the join already repairs.
- Zero-shot cloning tends to copy the reference's channel. A take whose SNR, C50 or high-frequency rolloff differs from the voice's median is likely a take where the model drifted in channel, and the fix is a retake with a new seed. Brouhaha gives SNR and C50 per frame cheaply on CPU (SincNet plus LSTM).
- DSP checks that need no model and map to fixes: (a) clipping, count samples within 0.1 dB of full scale or use adeclip, fix by gain before the join or retake. (b) hum, ratio of energy in narrow bands at 50 or 60 Hz and harmonics to the voice's median, fix by notch filter. (c) bandwidth, frequency below which 99% of energy lies (spectral rolloff) per take compared to the voice's median, fix by retake. (d) cut-off final phoneme, energy in the last 50 ms before the trim point relative to the take's active RMS, plus a check that the take's final 80 ms frame is not abruptly non-zero, fix by retake or by not trimming that edge. (e) breaths and gasps, unvoiced high-energy segments at take edges, fix by attenuating to a target as RX does.
- Per-voice outlier detection: build a feature vector per take (LUFS, SNR, C50, rolloff, spectral flatness, pitch median, MOS scores, speaker cosine) and flag takes beyond about 3 MAD from the voice's median on any axis. The voice's 220-take chapter gives enough samples for stable medians for the main voices but not for a voice with 2 lines.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that measures detection of neural-codec buzz in codec-language-model TTS specifically. The LAION detectors' accuracy on real TTS output is unknown.
- No source gave a validated hum or bandwidth-change detector for TTS. The DSP checks above are my design, not published methods.
- No literature was found on detecting a cut-off final phoneme at the signal level.

## 3. Loudness: what do BS.1770 and EBU R128 measure, with which tools, why does RMS differ from LUFS on speech, what do delivery specs ask, and what suits a car and bedtime listening?

### Takeaway
BS.1770 loudness is K-weighted (high-pass near 38 Hz plus a +4 dB presence shelf) and gated, so it tracks perceived speech loudness better than plain RMS. Speech delivery targets cluster at -18 LUFS (AES TD1008) to -16 LUFS (Apple, with -1 dBTP), and spoken word typically has a loudness range of 3 to 5 LU, with sources saying commuter listening needs a smaller range still. No source gives a numeric LRA for cars or for sleep.

### Cited Findings
- Momentary loudness integrates power over a sliding 400 ms window, short-term over 3 s, both ungated. Integrated loudness uses 400 ms blocks with an absolute gate at -70 LUFS and a relative gate 10 LU below the absolute-gated level. Loudness range (LRA) uses short-term values with a -70 LUFS absolute gate and a relative gate 20 LU below, and is the difference between the 10th and 95th percentiles - [Essentia LoudnessEBUR128](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_LoudnessEBUR128.html), [EBU Tech 3342 (2023 edition)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3342.pdf)
- EBU R128 guidance says LRA is not recommended for programmes shorter than 1 minute because there are too few data points - [EBU Tech 3342](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3342.pdf)
- EBU R128 normalizes programmes to -23 LUFS plus or minus 0.5 LU - [EBU loudness page](https://tech.ebu.ch/loudness)
- K-weighting has two stages, a high shelf of about +4 dB above roughly 1.5 kHz modelling the head, and a second-order high-pass (RLB) near 38 Hz. The ITU study behind it found the weighted mean-square Leq(RLB) performed best for monophonic signals - [Fora Soft K-weighting glossary](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/glossary/terms-audio/k-weighting), [TV Technology on BS.1770-1](https://tvtechnology.com/opinions/using-bs-17701-for-fun-and-profit), [oximedia-metering filter source](https://docs.rs/crate/oximedia-metering/latest/source/src/filters.rs), [ITU-R BS.1770-1 PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-1-200709-S!!PDF-E.pdf)
- Apple measures LKFS and true peak per ITU-R BS.1770-5 - [Apple Podcasts audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/audio-requirements)
- pyloudnorm (Christian Steinmetz, MIT) implements BS.1770-4. Version 0.2.0 was released 4 January 2026 and needs Python 3.9 or later - [GitHub csteinmetz1/pyloudnorm](https://github.com/csteinmetz1/pyloudnorm), [CERN PyPI mirror](https://simple-repository.app.cern.ch/project/pyloudnorm)
- ffmpeg loudnorm defaults: I -24 LUFS, LRA 7 LU, TP -2 dBTP. Linear mode needs measured_I, measured_LRA, measured_TP and measured_thresh from a first pass and falls back to dynamic mode if the target LRA is below the source LRA or if the gain would push true peak over the target. Dynamic mode upsamples to 192 kHz for true-peak detection, so the output rate must be set explicitly. A dual_mono option compensates mono files meant for stereo playback - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- iZotope RX Leveler's target is an average K-weighted RMS level and iZotope says it is not a loudness-compliant tool. Compliance is a separate Loudness module - [RX 6 Leveler help](https://downloads.izotope.com/docs/rx6/45-leveler/index.html), [RX 8 Leveler help](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx8/en/leveler/index.html)
- ACX requires each file between -23 and -18 dB RMS, peaks below -3 dB, noise floor below -60 dB RMS, 1 to 5 s of room tone at head and tail, MP3 CBR 192 kbps or more at 44.1 kHz, at most 120 minutes per file, and consistent levels, tone, noise and spacing. ACX uses RMS, not LUFS, and prohibits unauthorized TTS or AI narration - [ACX audio submission requirements](https://help.acx.com/s/article/acx-audio-submission-requirements)
- Apple Podcasts: about -16 LKFS plus or minus 1 dB, true peak not above -1 dBFS, preconditioned before encoding, AAC preferred for RSS. For constrained bit rates Apple suggests dropping 48 to 24 kHz or converting to mono - [Apple Podcasts audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/audio-requirements)
- AES TD1008 (2021, successor to TD1004 of 2015) sets a Distribution Loudness of -18 LUFS for speech-only and assorted content and -16 LUFS for music - [Production Advice on TD1008](https://productionadvice.co.uk/td1008/)
- Spotify's mastering FAQ recommends integrated loudness near -14 LUFS and true peak below -1 dB. Secondary sources disagree on whether its podcast target is -14 or -16 - [WBUR on platform loudness](https://www.wbur.org/citrus/2020/07/02/lufs-loudness-public-radio-npr-spotify-google)
- Auphonic: typical LRA is about 3 LU for news and about 5 LU for talks, LRA comparisons are meaningful only for pure speech, a show for train or car commuters should have an even smaller dynamic range, and the listening environment's "loudness comfort zone" sets how much range is usable. The -23 LUFS target does not perform well in noisy mobile environments - [Auphonic dynamic range processing](https://us.auphonic.com/blog/2019/08/19/dynamic-range-processing/), [Auphonic loudness targets for mobile](https://us.auphonic.com/blog/2013/01/07/loudness-targets-mobile-audio-podcasts-radio-tv/), [Auphonic dialog loudness](https://us.auphonic.com/blog/2020/10/08/dialog-loudness-normalization/)
- Dolby DRC Line mode holds dialogue at a constant -31 dBFS LeqA while allowing scaled low-level boost and high-level cut. RF mode applies its heavy profile fully with no user scaling. Consumer receivers expose these as Light and Heavy compression or a Night Mode for low-volume listening late at night - [Dolby DP569 manual, line and RF mode](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/76738/dolby-laboratories-dp569.html?page=65), [Dolby DP570 manual, DRC](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/73439/dolby-laboratories-dp570.html?page=100), [Sony receiver help](https://helpguide.sony.net/ha/strza81/v1/en/contents/TP0001221256.html)
- In-vehicle studies: higher cabin SPL from engine and road noise is not necessarily associated with lower speech intelligibility (STI), the Speech Intelligibility Index tracked STI best among metrics compared, and low SNR raises listening effort. A 2024 Fraunhofer IDMT study found preferred volume and EQ shift with driving noise - [In-vehicle speech intelligibility metrics](https://www.inderscience.com/filter.php?aid=43193), [In-vehicle STI study](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/811643491854581760), [HEAD acoustics DAGA 2015](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/DAGA_2015/data/articles/000272.pdf), [Fraunhofer IDMT 2024](https://idmt.fraunhofer.de/en/Press_and_Media/press_releases/2024/more-than-just-loud-bass.html)

### Inferences
- RMS vs LUFS on audiogen's voices: K-weighting discounts energy below about 100 Hz and adds about 4 dB above 1.5 kHz, so at equal -18 dB RMS a deep, bass-heavy voice measures quieter in LUFS (and sounds quieter) than a bright voice. The current per-voice median RMS can therefore leave voices a few dB apart perceptually. Leveling each voice to a LUFS-style value (K-weighted, gated active-speech loudness) would fix this with the same code shape.
- BS.1770 integrated loudness on a single 1 to 3 s take rests on only a few 400 ms blocks, and LRA is not defined for programmes under a minute. A per-take measure should therefore be the K-weighted mean power over active frames (or ungated K-weighted power over the trimmed take), not integrated LUFS with gating, and loudness range should be measured per chapter.
- Mono vs stereo: BS.1770 sums channel powers, so the same mono signal duplicated to two channels reads about 3 LU louder. A mono Ogg Opus at -19 LUFS (mono measurement) corresponds to about -16 LUFS dual-mono, which is what Apple's -16 LKFS on a two-channel file means. ffmpeg's dual_mono option exists for this.
- Target suggestion for the report writer: -18 LUFS integrated measured as mono (TD1008's speech target) with true peak at or below -1 dBTP, and a chapter LRA of about 4 to 6 LU for a drama with whispers and shouts (between news and talk norms, with the car argument pulling lower). This is a synthesis, not a published spec.
- For bed listening the danger is a loud line after quiet ones. Dolby's Line-mode idea (dialogue constant, boost low, cut high) is the right model: cap how far a whisper may sit below and a shout above the voice's normal level, rather than removing the difference.

### Gaps
- No standard or study gives a numeric loudness range or dynamics target for in-car speech listening or for falling asleep. The 3 to 5 LU figures are Auphonic's typical values for news and talk, not car targets.
- The EBU Tech 3341 PDF could not be parsed here, so meter definitions are cited from Essentia and Tech 3342. The ffmpeg ebur128 filter and libebur128 docs were not read.
- No source quantifies the RMS vs LUFS difference for speech of different timbres. The inference above is from the filter shape.
- Audible's own (non-ACX) loudness rules for listeners were not found beyond ACX's RMS spec.

## 4. Leveling: how do dialogue levelers work, how can lines be leveled take by take without flattening an intended whisper or shout, and how are swings inside one take detected?

### Takeaway
Commercial levelers (Auphonic, iZotope RX Leveler) classify speech vs background, ride gain slowly toward a K-weighted target and leave breaths and background unamplified, while ffmpeg offers frame-based riders (dynaudnorm, speechnorm) and static tools (compand, alimiter). For a TTS drama the cleanest design is per-take static gain from a measured loudness toward a voice target offset by the line's intended intensity, followed by a gentle chapter-level compressor and limiter, with momentary loudness used to flag swings inside a take.

### Cited Findings
- Auphonic's Adaptive Leveler uses machine learning to classify segments as music, background or speech, does not amplify background segments (noise, wind, breathing, silence), balances level differences between speakers and between speech and music, applies dynamic range compression to speech, and gives music gentler treatment. Earlier versions were based on the LRA descriptor, and Auphonic later built its own dynamic range measure. Global loudness normalization is a separate constant-gain step measured per BS.1770 - [Auphonic features](https://auphonic.com/features), [Auphonic Leveler](https://auphonic.com/leveler), [Auphonic leveler customization](https://auphonic.com/blog/2018/11/05/auphonic-leveler-customization/)
- Auphonic's dialog loudness feature classifies speech vs non-speech and reports separate loudness and LRA for each, and in its example the Adaptive Leveler produced a much smaller overall and dialog LRA - [Auphonic dialog loudness](https://us.auphonic.com/blog/2020/10/08/dialog-loudness-normalization/)
- iZotope RX Leveler targets an average K-weighted RMS level, may not reach high targets without clipping, and includes breath control based on harmonic structure that applies clip gain to breaths - [RX 8 Leveler help](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx8/en/leveler/index.html), [RX 6 Leveler help](https://downloads.izotope.com/docs/rx6/45-leveler/index.html)
- ffmpeg dynaudnorm adjusts gain per frame without compressing dynamics: framelen 500 ms, gausssize 31 frames (odd, 3 to 301), peak 0.95, maxgain 10, targetrms off by default, compress 0 (off), threshold 0 - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- ffmpeg speechnorm expands or compresses each half-cycle toward a target peak (default 0.95), with expansion and compression factors (default 2), raise and fall rates (default 0.001), an optional RMS target and a link option. Documented presets run from weak and slow (e=3, r=0.00001, l=1) to extreme and fast (e=50, r=0.0001, l=1) - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- ffmpeg compand takes a transfer function as points with typical attack 0.3 s and decay 0.8 s and a soft knee (default 0.01 dB). alimiter is a lookahead limiter (attack 5 ms, release 50 ms) whose level option is enabled by default and normalizes the output back to 0 dB. agate is a downward or upward gate (threshold 0.125, ratio 2, attack 20 ms, release 250 ms, range 0.06125) - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- Momentary loudness (400 ms) and short-term loudness (3 s) are the BS.1770 descriptors for loudness over time - [Essentia LoudnessEBUR128](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_LoudnessEBUR128.html)
- Dolby Line mode keeps dialogue at a constant level while boosting low passages and cutting high ones by profile - [Dolby DP569 manual](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/76738/dolby-laboratories-dp569.html?page=65)

### Inferences
- dynaudnorm's Gaussian over 31 frames of 500 ms smooths gain over about 15 s, so it behaves as a slow rider across many lines. That evens out chapters but will pull a whispered line up toward its neighbours, which is the flattening AJ wants to avoid. speechnorm works on half-cycles and is too fast and too colouring for drama.
- alimiter's default level=1 renormalizes to 0 dBFS, which would undo a loudness target if used as a final limiter without level=0. Worth a note for whoever builds the chain.
- Take-by-take leveling that keeps intent: (1) measure each take's active K-weighted loudness L_take. (2) set the voice's normal target T_voice (for example -18 LUFS-style). (3) derive the intended offset from the script direction (whisper, normal, raised, shout) as a bounded value, for example whisper -6 LU, soft -3, normal 0, raised +2, shout +3. (4) apply gain T_voice + offset - L_take, clamped to plus or minus 6 dB, so a take that came out quieter than its direction asks for is fixed while a whisper stays a whisper. The offsets are my proposal and need listening tests.
- Where there is no direction, a whispered or shouted take can be recognized from the signal (lower K-weighted loudness with high spectral flatness for whisper, raised F0 and loudness for shout), and the same bounded rule applies.
- Swing inside one take: compute momentary loudness every 100 ms over active frames and flag when the range (95th minus 10th percentile, mirroring LRA) exceeds about 8 to 10 LU, or when any 400 ms window jumps more than about 6 LU from its neighbour. The fix is either a retake (if the swing is a model glitch) or a slow in-take gain envelope. The thresholds are guesses to calibrate on flagged takes.
- After per-take gains, a gentle chapter compressor (for example 2:1 above the normal level with slow attack and release) plus a true-peak limiter at -1 dBTP caps shouts for bed and car without touching normal lines.

### Gaps
- No published method or threshold was found for detecting loudness swings inside one TTS take.
- Open-source vocal riders beyond ffmpeg were not found in this pass.
- Auphonic does not publish its leveler's algorithm, and its claims are self-reported.

## 5. Speaker consistency: which embeddings suit comparing a take with the reference and with the voice's other takes, what thresholds are typical, and do they catch drift in timbre, gender or accent?

### Takeaway
The TTS field standard is cosine similarity from a WavLM-large speaker-verification model (Seed-TTS-eval), where real human pairs score about 0.73, but many ONNX models (CAM++, ERes2NetV2, WeSpeaker ResNets, TitaNet) run in sherpa-onnx on CPU and are fine for relative checks. Thresholds are encoder-specific and must be calibrated per voice, and speaker embeddings demonstrably miss accent mismatches that listeners hear.

### Cited Findings
- Seed-TTS-eval computes SIM as cosine similarity between WavLM-large (fine-tuned for speaker verification) embeddings of each test utterance and its reference clip - [GitHub BytedanceSpeech/seed-tts-eval](https://github.com/BytedanceSpeech/seed-tts-eval)
- Human ground truth on Seed-TTS test-en scores SIM about 0.73 with WavLM (0.734 in one paper, with ERes2Net giving 0.742) - [WavTTS, arXiv 2606.03455](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.03455), [Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS, arXiv 2607.23938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.23938)
- ZipVoice's evaluation uses an ECAPA-TDNN plus WavLM model, and guides warn not to mix SIM numbers from different encoders - [ZipVoice evaluation guide](https://www.besthub.dev/articles/building-an-open-source-tts-evaluation-framework-with-zipvoice-omnivoice-latest-benchmarks-d571124e43c9)
- SpeechBrain spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb: 0.80% EER on VoxCeleb1-test (cleaned), released 5 March 2021, cosine scoring. The API's verify_batch default threshold is 0.25, and maintainers advise calibrating on your own data - [HF speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb](https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb), [SpeechBrain API](https://speechbrain.readthedocs.io/en/v1.0.2/API/speechbrain.inference.speaker.html), [SpeechBrain discussion 2148](https://github.com/speechbrain/speechbrain/discussions/2148)
- In SIG-VC's setup an ECAPA-TDNN cosine of about 0.6 separated same-speaker from different-speaker pairs - [SIG-VC, arXiv 2111.03811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.03811)
- A 2020 multi-speaker TTS study found Pearson r = 0.89 between embedding cosine similarity and listening-test speaker similarity - [arXiv 2011.04839](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.04839)
- ClonEval uses WavLM for cloning similarity and notes that relying only on neural evaluation can misalign with human perception - [ClonEval, arXiv 2504.20581](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.20581v3)
- Yang, Sun, Rose, Lyu (IEEE SLT 2026, arXiv April 2026, v3 September 2026): listeners rated clones of accented speakers less similar than clones of standard speakers, but the gap in several speaker-embedding spaces vanished after adjusting for each speaker's within-original variability. They conclude accent preservation is not captured by off-the-shelf speaker embeddings - [arXiv 2604.01562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01562)
- sherpa-onnx speaker embedding models in ONNX include 3D-Speaker CAM++, ERes2Net and ERes2NetV2, WeSpeaker ResNet34, 152, 221, 293 and CAM++, and NeMo TitaNet, with files from about 26 MB to 221 MB - [HF csukuangfj/speaker-embedding-models](https://huggingface.co/csukuangfj/speaker-embedding-models/tree/main), [sherpa-onnx model docs](https://csukuangfj.github.io/sherpa/onnx/speaker-diarization/models.html)
- 3D-Speaker provides ERes2Net, ERes2NetV2 and CAM++ with ONNX export - [3D-Speaker toolkit, arXiv 2403.19971](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.19971)
- An unofficial int8 ERes2NetV2 is 28 MB (71 MB fp32), 192-dimensional at 16 kHz, and needs sherpa metadata kept to load - [HF phoenix124/kept-models](https://huggingface.co/phoenix124/kept-models)
- ReDimNet (July 2024): a family from 1 to 15M parameters and 0.5 to 20 GMACs (B0 to B6), with leaderboard EERs such as 0.39% for a 9.2M B5 variant. ReDimNet2 (arXiv March 2026) reaches 0.287% EER on Vox1-O at 12.3M parameters. No ONNX export was found - [ReDimNet, arXiv 2407.18223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18223), [ReDimNet2, arXiv 2603.11841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11841), [leaderboard](https://paperswithcode2.com/benchmark/52098)

### Inferences
- Use two comparisons per take: (a) cosine to the reference clip, and (b) cosine to the centroid of that voice's accepted takes. (b) is the stronger drift signal because the reference clip differs in channel and style from directed takes, and whispered or shouted takes will sit lower against the reference for legitimate reasons.
- Thresholds: take the per-voice distribution of (b) and flag takes more than about 3 MAD below the median, with an absolute floor around the encoder's same-speaker boundary (around 0.5 to 0.6 cosine for ECAPA-class models, encoder-specific). A flagged take gets a retake with a new seed, or, with N seeds rendered, the seed with the highest centroid similarity wins.
- ERes2NetV2 or CAM++ through sherpa-onnx fits the existing audio Python (sherpa-onnx is already installed) and runs on CPU beside the GPU render. WavLM-large SV is the field reference but is a heavier PyTorch model.
- Gender flips should produce large cosine drops in any of these encoders, but accent drift will not be caught reliably, per Yang et al. Accent drift is better caught by other researchers' text and ASR checks, which are out of this note's scope.

### Gaps
- No source gave a calibrated threshold for detecting timbre or gender drift within one cloned voice.
- CPU speed of the sherpa-onnx embedding models on the M1 or the laptop was not found.
- No ReDimNet ONNX export or sherpa-onnx support was confirmed.

## 6. Restoration as post-processing: which tools exist, what are the risks on synthetic speech, and what applies at Breeze TTS 2's output rate?

### Takeaway
Breeze TTS 2 outputs 24 kHz, so content stops near 12 kHz, and the realistic restoration options are bandwidth extension to 48 kHz (AP-BWE, MossFormer2_SR_48K, NovaSR, FlashSR) and light denoising (DeepFilterNet3), not full generative restoration. Generative restorers (Sidon, Miipher-2, Resemble Enhance, VoiceFixer) re-synthesize the voice through their own vocoders and can drift speaker identity or content in ways MOS predictors do not catch, so on already-clean synthetic speech they add risk for little gain.

### Cited Findings
- Breeze TTS 2: 3B parameters, released 25 August 2026, streaming API outputs mono 24 kHz signed 16-bit little-endian PCM, audio tokenizer based on Qwen3-TTS, code Apache 2.0, weights under the BreezeBlue Research and Non-Commercial License - [HF BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2](https://huggingface.co/BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2)
- Breeze-TTS-2.cpp: "Bilingual English and Mandarin, 24 kHz, about 2x realtime at Q8_0 on an RTX 3060". Quants f16 5.9 GB, q8_0 3.3 GB, q6_k 2.9 GB, q4_k 2.4 GB plus experimental depth-decoder quants - [HF HoppouAI/Breeze-TTS-2.cpp](https://huggingface.co/HoppouAI/Breeze-TTS-2.cpp)
- DeepFilterNet3: full-band 48 kHz with 10 ms hops, real-time factor 0.19 single-threaded on an Intel i5-8250U in the original work, ONNX archive shipped upstream, dual MIT and Apache-2.0 per mirrors - [GitHub Rikorose/DeepFilterNet](https://github.com/rikorose/deepfilternet), [DeepFilterNet3 paper, arXiv 2305.08227](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.08227.pdf), [soniqo DeepFilterNet3-ONNX](https://huggingface.co/soniqo/DeepFilterNet3-ONNX)
- Resemble Enhance (December 2023): a UNet denoiser plus an enhancer for quality and bandwidth, trained on 44.1 kHz speech, MIT licence - [GitHub resemble-ai/resemble-enhance](https://www.github.com/resemble-ai/resemble-enhance), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/resemble-enhance), [Resemble page](https://www.resemble.ai/?p=249174)
- VoiceFixer (2021 to 2022): ResUNet mel restoration plus a TFGAN vocoder, restores noise, reverberation and clipping and extends low-bandwidth speech to 44.1 kHz. MIT per a distro package spec - [VoiceFixer, arXiv 2204.05841](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.05841), [arXiv 2109.13731](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.13731), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/voicefixer/0.0.9)
- Miipher-2 (Google DeepMind, arXiv 7 May 2025): frozen 2B-parameter USM encoder, 20M-parameter parallel adapters, WaveFit vocoder, RTF 0.0078 on a TPU v4i, built to clean million-hour datasets. Not released - [arXiv 2505.04457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04457v1), [Sidon paper noting USM is closed](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17052v2)
- Sidon (Nakata, Saito, Ueda, Saruwatari, arXiv 21 September 2025, ICASSP 2026): an open w2v-BERT 2.0 (600M) feature cleanser plus vocoder, up to 500x realtime on one GPU, comparable to Miipher, code and model released. A secondary summary says it outputs 48 kHz through HiFi-GAN. A Core ML port exists - [arXiv 2509.17052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17052), [Sidon HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17052v2), [HF aufklarer/Sidon-CoreML](https://huggingface.co/aufklarer/Sidon-CoreML)
- AP-BWE (arXiv January 2024): parallel amplitude and phase prediction for bandwidth extension, fully convolutional, HiFi-GAN-based, with 16 kHz and 48 kHz target models. A secondary source claims about 292x realtime at 48 kHz on an RTX 4090 (unverified) - [GitHub yxlu-0102/AP-BWE](https://github.com/yxlu-0102/AP-BWE), [arXiv 2401.06387](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06387v2)
- FlashSR (KAIST, arXiv January 2025): one-step distilled diffusion from any rate between 4 and 32 kHz to 48 kHz for speech, music and effects, about 22x faster than AudioSR, about 0.36 s per 5.12 s on one GPU. No official code repository was found - [arXiv 2501.10807](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10807)
- NovaSR: about 52 KB, under 10 conv1d layers with Snake activations, 16 to 48 kHz, self-reported 3600x realtime on an A100, trained on about 100 hours (mls_sidon plus VCTK), Apache 2.0, author says still training - [GitHub NovaSR](https://github.com/MaxMax2016/NovaSR), [SourceForge mirror](https://sourceforge.net/mirror/novasr)
- ClearerVoice-Studio MossFormer2_SR_48K: input of at least 16 kHz effective rate, output 48 kHz, MossFormer2 generator plus HiFi-GAN, Apache-2.0, trained on clean speech (run MossFormer2_SE_48K first on noisy input) - [HF alibabasglab/MossFormer2_SR_48K](https://huggingface.co/alibabasglab/MossFormer2_SR_48K), [ClearerVoice-Studio, arXiv 2506.19398](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2506.19398)
- Generative enhancement hallucinates in two ways, linguistic (wrong content) and acoustic (speaker characteristics drift), especially under heavy noise. DiTSE reports better identity and content preservation than prior generative enhancers - [PASE, AAAI 2026](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/40562/44523), [DiTSE, arXiv 2504.09381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09381)
- LAION's spectral_denoising detector targets the metallic or underwater sound that spectral gating (noisereduce) produces - [HF laion/speech-artifact-detectors](https://huggingface.co/laion/speech-artifact-detectors)
- Repo check: audiogen's breeze.py reads the sample rate from breeze-server's /health and defaults to 24000 (D:\fish\serial_audio\breeze.py line 335), and fx.py resamples to 48 kHz for Opus (fx.py lines 87 to 88)

### Inferences
- Breeze's 24 kHz output is already clean speech, so denoisers have little to remove and mainly risk adding artifacts. DeepFilterNet3 at 48 kHz is the low-risk option if a reference clip's noise was cloned into takes, and only for takes flagged by a BAK or SNR outlier check.
- Bandwidth extension from 12 kHz to 20 kHz adds air and sibilance detail. In a car with road noise and at low volume in bed that band is hard to hear, so its value is small. If used, AP-BWE 48 kHz or MossFormer2_SR_48K apply to every take equally, which keeps voices matched. Run it after the join on the whole chapter, then re-check speaker similarity on a sample.
- Generative restorers (Sidon, Resemble Enhance, VoiceFixer) are best kept as a repair of last resort for a take that keeps failing quality checks across seeds, never as a blanket pass, and their output should be re-checked with the speaker-similarity check because MOS predictors do not catch identity drift.
- CPU feasibility: DeepFilterNet3 (RTF 0.19 single-thread) and NovaSR run on CPU. AP-BWE and MossFormer2 are small GAN generators that should run on CPU slower than realtime-multiples quoted on GPUs. Sidon (600M encoder) and FlashSR are GPU tools and would contend with Breeze on the laptop.

### Gaps
- No study was found that applies these restorers to clean zero-shot TTS output and measures the effect on speaker similarity or MOS.
- CPU speeds for AP-BWE, MossFormer2_SR_48K, Resemble Enhance and Sidon were not found.
- Sidon's code and weight licence and exact output rate were not confirmed on primary pages.
- Cloud alternatives: Auphonic is free for 2 hours of processed audio a month, with recurring plans listed at about 9 h for $11 up to 100 h for $99 a month, roughly $1 to $1.2 per hour, though third-party trackers list higher prices - [Auphonic pricing](https://auphonic.com/pricing), [Auphonic pricing FAQ](https://auphonic.com/help/web/pricing_faq.html). Other cloud restorers and their per-hour costs were not researched.

## 7. The join: room tone vs digital silence, crossfades, pause length by punctuation and speaker change, de-essing and EQ across voices, Opus bitrate for speech

### Takeaway
Audiobook practice fills gaps with room tone rather than digital silence (ACX wants a noise floor below -60 dB RMS but 1 to 5 s of room tone at file edges), read-speech studies put sentence pauses around 0.2 to 1.4 s and paragraph pauses around 0.4 to 1.8 s, and Opus is essentially transparent for mono speech at about 32 kbps (28 to 40 kbps recommended for fullband mono).

### Cited Findings
- ACX: 1 to 5 s of room tone at head and tail, no more than 5 s, noise floor below -60 dB RMS, and consistent spacing and noise across the book - [ACX requirements](https://help.acx.com/s/article/acx-audio-submission-requirements)
- An ACX passage quoted on the Adobe forum says many encoders struggle with absolute silence and may substitute their own noise floor - [Adobe Audition forum](https://community.adobe.com/t5/audition-discussions/audiobook-voice-sample-feedback-welcome/td-p/10831933)
- Practitioner advice: complete silence between passages can jolt the listener, editors splice clean room tone over removed noises and pickups, removing noise can create a more noticeable pattern than leaving stationary low noise, and hard edges or mismatched room tone feel edited even without a click, so use slightly longer fades when crossing between different noise beds - [Podfeet blog](https://www.podfeet.com/blog/?p=36177), [Scribe Media audiobook editing](https://scribemedia.com/blog/audiobook-editing)
- A 2020 read-speech study (Respectus Philologicus) found sentence pauses of about 200 to 980 ms for fast readers and 340 to 1360 ms for slow readers, paragraph pauses of 400 to 1340 ms (fast) and 750 to 1800 ms (slow), with paragraph pauses about 1.2 times longer after long paragraphs and sentence pauses up to 1.4 times longer after long sentences (abstract only) - [CEEOL article](https://ceeol.com/search/article-detail?id=871661)
- Google published a study of raters' sensitivity to inter-sentence pause durations in American English (findings not read) - [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/a-study-of-raters-sensitivity-to-inter-sentence-pause-durations-in-american-english-speech/)
- TTS vendor pause presets: WellSaid 0.3 s small, 0.8 s natural, 1.2 s long - [WellSaid help](https://help.wellsaid.io/hc/en-us/articles/39050121654931-Adding-Longer-Pauses-Between-Sentences)
- ffmpeg deesser options: intensity i (default 0), treble ducking m (0.5), frequency keep f (0.5), output mode s - [FFmpeg filters](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html)
- Opus: 28 to 40 kbps recommended for fullband mono, 48 to 72 kbps for stereo - [Valin, Opus SILK paper AES 135, via search snippet](https://jmvalin.ca/papers/aes135_opus_silk.pdf). Hydrogenaudio rates 32 kbps as essentially transparent for speech and suitable for audiobooks, with 24 kbps near-transparent - [Hydrogenaudio Opus wiki](https://wiki.hydrogenaud.io/?title=Opus)
- Apple suggests 24 kHz and mono when bit rates are constrained, with mono MP3 at 64 to 128 kbps recommended for 44.1 or 48 kHz - [Apple Podcasts audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/audio-requirements)

### Inferences
- audiogen's 0.3 s of digital silence between takes sits at the short end of sentence pauses and at the floor of the vendor presets. A pause rule by context would be: same speaker, same paragraph about 0.35 to 0.5 s, sentence end about 0.5 to 0.8 s, speaker change about 0.4 to 0.7 s (dialogue turns in drama run tighter than narration), paragraph or scene change about 0.9 to 1.5 s. These are a synthesis of the read-speech ranges, not published audiobook norms.
- Digital silence vs room tone: Breeze takes have a very low noise floor, so the step from a take's tail to digital zero is probably inaudible in a car. In bed on headphones at low volume a step can be heard if a voice's cloned reference carried audible noise. A constant, very low comfort noise bed (for example around -70 dBFS, under ACX's -60 dB floor) under the whole chapter, or keeping each take's own tail instead of cutting to zero, would remove gating steps. The 3 ms fades are short. Fades of 10 to 20 ms on tails into silence are more common practice, but no source was found giving a number.
- De-essing and EQ across voices: Breeze stops near 12 kHz, so sibilance energy is in the 5 to 10 kHz band. A per-voice check of the 5 to 10 kHz band energy against the chapter median, with a dynamic de-esser only on outlier voices, keeps voices matched. This is a design suggestion, unsourced.
- Opus: mono at 32 kbps is a well-supported setting, 24 kbps saves space with near-transparency, and 40 kbps adds margin. Opus encodes at 48 kHz internally, so the existing 48 kHz resample is correct.

### Gaps
- No audiobook or audio-drama mastering guide was found that gives crossfade lengths, pause length by punctuation or speaker change, or EQ matching across voices.
- The Xiph Opus Recommended Settings wiki failed to load (expired certificate), so Opus figures come from Hydrogenaudio and the Valin paper via search.
- No source tested Opus transparency at each bitrate on audiobook speech.
